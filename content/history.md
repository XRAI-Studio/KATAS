## One heart, one mind

Isshin Ryu means "one heart way". It was founded on Okinawa by Tatsuo Shimabuku
(1908–1975), who named the style in January 1956 after decades of training in the two
great Okinawan traditions and in weapons. The name is a promise about how the style is
meant to be practised: with the whole heart, without holding back.

## The founder

Shimabuku was born Shinkichi Shimabuku in Chan village (now Kyan), Okinawa, and later took
the name Tatsuo, "dragon man". He began karate as a boy under his uncle, then trained in
Shorin-ryu with Chotoku Kyan, one of the most respected fighters of the era, and later
with Choki Motobu, famous for his practical close-range fighting. He also studied Goju-ryu
under its founder, Chojun Miyagi, and Okinawan weapons under Taira Shinken.

By the 1940s he was teaching his own blend. Students remember him as a small, quick man
who was as interested in what worked as in what was traditional.

## The vision of the Mizu Gami

The symbol of Isshin Ryu is the Mizu Gami, the water goddess: a woman who is half sea
serpent, shown calm in a storm, with a raised open hand. Shimabuku said she came to him in
a dream in the 1950s and that the image showed him what his style should be. The open hand
is peace; the fist behind is strength kept in reserve. The three stars above her are said
to stand for Shimabuku's teachers, or for mind, body and spirit, depending on who tells it.
(check: your dojo's telling of the symbol)

## What makes Isshin Ryu different

- **The vertical fist.** Punches are thrown with the fist upright and the thumb on top,
  not twisted over. Shimabuku taught that it is faster, keeps the wrist stronger, and
  suits close range.
- **Muscle blocks.** Blocks land with the fleshy side of the forearm, not the bone, so the
  block can absorb and redirect without injuring the arm.
- **Snap kicks.** Kicks snap out and back rather than lock out, keeping the leg safe from
  grabs and the kicker on balance.
- **Natural stances.** Stances are higher and more mobile than in many Japanese styles,
  built for moving and turning rather than posing.
- **Kata as fighting.** Every kata movement is expected to have a bunkai, an application
  against a real attacker.

## The kata

Isshin Ryu keeps a short list of empty-hand kata so each can be studied deeply:

| Kata | Where it came from | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seisan | Shorin-ryu | Forward pressure, block-punch-kick combinations, the first full kata |
| Seiunchin | Goju-ryu | Rooted stances, close-range control, no kicks |
| Naihanchi | Shorin-ryu | Fighting sideways on a line, elbows, defending sweeps |
| Wansu | Shorin-ryu | Strong linear strikes, angles, the signature dump |
| Chinto | Shorin-ryu | Diagonal movement, one-leg stances, arm control, fighting on a slope |
| Kusanku | Shorin-ryu | Night fighting, the longest and most complex kata |
| Sunsu | Shimabuku's own | The master's summary of the whole style |
| Sanchin | Goju-ryu | Breathing, tension, the body as one piece |

Plus weapons kata for the bo, sai and tonfa, inherited from Taira Shinken and the Okinawan
kobudo tradition.

## The Marines and the road to America

Isshin Ryu spread further and faster than most Okinawan styles for one reason: the United
States Marines. From the mid-1950s Shimabuku taught servicemen stationed on Okinawa, many of
whom trained daily for a year or more and then carried the style home. Early students such
as Don Nagle, Harold Long, Steve Armstrong and Harold Mitchum opened dojo across the
United States, and Shimabuku himself visited America in 1964 and 1966. Today there are
more Isshin Ryu practitioners outside Okinawa than on it.

## The code

Shimabuku gave his students a set of precepts, the Kenpo Gokui, to think about alongside
their training. They are short sayings rather than rules, such as: a person's heart is the
same as heaven and earth; the manner of drinking and spitting is either hard or soft; the
eye must see all sides; the ear must listen in all directions. Ask your teacher what each
one means; the answers change as you do.

## After the founder

Shimabuku died in 1975. Leadership of the style passed through his family and senior
students, and Isshin Ryu today exists as several associations rather than one, each keeping
the kata and principles above while differing in details of the forms. That is why the
viewer marks some movements as "reconstructed" and why your dojo's version always rules.

## Why we study it here

At Travel Schooling, kata is a memory game, a balance game and a history lesson at once.
Learning Seisan means learning to look before you turn. Learning Naihanchi means learning
to keep your base when someone tries to take it. And learning where these movements came
from means learning that a hundred years of teachers argued, adapted and kept what worked,
which is what we hope you will do too.
